🤖 A Plain-English Guide to AI

What AI actually is: software that performs tasks which normally require human judgement — recognising speech or images, finding patterns in data, translating language, or generating text, images and audio.

A short history

  • Alan Turing (1950) proposed the "Turing Test" as a way to ask whether a machine's behaviour could be indistinguishable from a human's — his wartime code-breaking work and mathematical logic underpin much of modern computing.
  • 1956 — Dartmouth Conference: mathematician John McCarthy coined the term "Artificial Intelligence," kicking off decades of research into logic and reasoning systems.
  • 1990s–2010s: the field shifted toward machine learning — systems that learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules.
  • 2012 onward: deep learning (large neural networks) drove major gains in image and speech recognition, followed by the generative AI wave from 2022 onward (large language models, image generators, and similar tools).

Types of AI — and a common misconception

  • Narrow AI (what exists today): systems built for a specific task — search, translation, recommendation engines, chatbots, image recognition. Very capable within their lane, but they don't generalise beyond it.
  • General AI (AGI): a hypothetical system matching human-level ability across arbitrary tasks. This does not exist yet — it's a research goal, not a current product, whatever marketing language might imply.
  • Superintelligence: a further hypothetical stage beyond AGI, discussed mainly in research and policy circles, not something currently being built.

Where it helps — and where it needs care

  • Healthcare: assisting with reading scans, flagging anomalies, and accelerating early-stage drug research (always alongside, not instead of, clinicians).
  • Cities: traffic and transport modelling, energy efficiency, and air-quality monitoring.
  • Work: drafting, summarising, and data analysis at speed — with human review still needed for accuracy.
  • Real risks: biased outputs from biased training data, privacy exposure, convincing deepfakes, and job disruption in some sectors. UK bodies including the AI Security Institute focus on testing systems for safety before wide deployment.

This is a general overview, not investment, legal or medical advice.

🔥 Trending & Viral — With a Fact-Check Habit

Viral stories spread because they're interesting, not because they're verified. Our approach: note what's actually confirmed by a named, checkable source, and clearly flag anything that's still just claim-and-counterclaim.

How to read a viral story critically

  • Check whether the original claim has a named source, not just "people are saying."
  • Look for whether other independent outlets have confirmed it, or whether it's one unverified post being reshared.
  • Be extra cautious with images and video — deepfakes and out-of-context clips are now common in viral content.

🕵️ Unsolved Cases & Popular Theories

These are genuinely interesting because they're unresolved — but "unresolved" is different from "anything goes." We separate the documented record from the theories built on top of it.

London & UK

  • Jack the Ripper (1888): at least five murders in Whitechapel are attributed to the same unidentified killer. The police investigation, victim details, and dozens of named suspects are documented history — the killer's identity is not, and never has been, confirmed.
  • Reported ghost sightings at the Tower of London and Hampton Court Palace are longstanding local folklore, not verified phenomena.

Internationally known

  • Bermuda Triangle: the disappearance rate in that region is not statistically unusual compared to other heavily trafficked waters — most mainstream investigations point to weather, navigation error, and heavy traffic rather than anything exotic.
  • MH370 (2014): the aircraft's disappearance is confirmed; a small amount of debris was later recovered; the main wreckage and definitive cause have never been found despite extensive searches.
  • Dyatlov Pass Incident (1959): nine hikers died in the Ural Mountains under circumstances that remain debated; a 2021 scientific study proposed a slab-avalanche explanation, though not every detail is universally accepted.

Presented for curiosity and critical thinking — not as confirmation of any theory beyond what's cited.

🔬 Science Facts Worth Knowing

Space & physics

  • Light travels at about 299,792 km per second in a vacuum — fast enough to circle the Earth roughly 7.5 times in one second.

Nature & biology

  • Properly sealed honey essentially never spoils — its low moisture and acidity prevent microbial growth, which is why edible honey has been found in millennia-old Egyptian tombs.
  • An octopus has three hearts and blue, copper-based blood; two hearts pump blood to the gills, one to the rest of the body.

Earth & engineering

  • Mount Everest gains roughly 4mm in height most years due to ongoing tectonic uplift from the Indian and Eurasian plates colliding.
  • The River Thames runs about 346km from its source in Gloucestershire to the North Sea; the stretch through central London is tidal, rising and falling twice a day.
